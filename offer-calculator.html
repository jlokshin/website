Will you make more money in big tech or the hot startup?

Money shouldn't be the only reason to choose a job. But, comp can be confusing and you should understand the math before making a decision.

Enter your offers to see what each is worth per year, and how big the startup's exit has to be before you come out ahead.

How it's calculated. All figures are pre-tax and per year, averaged over the years you expect to stay. Public: base + bonus + signing bonus ÷ years + RSUs, with RSUs changing in value each year by the expected stock growth you set. Startup: base + bonus + signing bonus ÷ years + options. Options vest over 4 years with a 1-year cliff. For each exit scenario, the future share price is today's price × the exit multiple, reduced by dilution and the preference/illiquidity haircut. Option value is vested options × (future price − strike), or zero if that's negative. The scenarios are probability-weighted, discounted back to today, and divided by the years you stay.

Preset assumptions. Startup dilution: 3% a year. Each funding round typically sells 15–25% of the company; spread across the time between rounds, that's roughly 8–10% a year early on and 2–5% for late-stage companies. Preference haircut: 10%. Investors' preferred stock is paid back before common stock (what you get), which barely matters in a big exit but can wipe out common in a small one; this is a judgment call, not an industry average. Discount rate: 8% a year. Roughly what the same money would earn in a stock index fund; the chance the startup fails is already in your exit odds, so this only covers waiting years to get paid.

Caveats. This is a rough model, not tax or financial advice. It ignores taxes (including ISO/AMT and 83(b) elections), liquidation-preference waterfalls, refreshers, exercise windows after you leave, and the gap between the 409A price and the preferred price. The probabilities you enter matter most, so use it to compare the offers fairly and to frame a negotiation, not as a forecast.
